A picture-book tour of a brand-new AI paper

The robot who suddenly got it

A tiny computer brain learns to do clock math. For thousands of tries it only memorizes answers. Then, all at once, it understands. Scientists call this grokking. A new paper explains why it happens, and we rebuilt their whole experiment ourselves to check.

I'm adding on a clock with 23 hours. Watch!

Paper: "A Spectral Theory of Grokking" by Pracher, de Jong, Lieshaus, Jeffares and Rulands (September 2026). Our rebuild ran on an ordinary 4-core computer, no graphics card.

Chapter 1 · The puzzle

Adding on a clock that only goes up to 22

On this clock, after 22 comes 0 again. So 9 + 17 isn't 26. You go around the clock and land on 3.

There are 23 × 23 = 529 of these puzzles. The robot gets to practice on 368 of them (orange squares). The other 161 are kept secret for a surprise quiz (blue outlines).

The practice puzzles are picked in neat diagonal stripes, so the clock's pattern is fair: no number is special.

Chapter 2 · The strange part

First it memorizes. Much later, it understands.

The homework score shoots up fast: the robot remembers every practice answer within a few hundred tries.

But the quiz score stays near zero for thousands of tries. It hasn't learned the rule, only the answers.

Then, around try 5,000, the quiz score climbs. By try 8,300 it gets 9 out of 10 surprise puzzles right. Nobody changed anything. That is grokking.

Chapter 3 · Why it happens

The tidy-up rule leaves tiny mistakes behind

Every time the robot practices, a tidy-up rule shrinks everything in its memory a little bit. It's like a backpack that slowly empties itself.

Because of that, the robot can never memorize its homework perfectly. A few tiny mistakes always stay.

Those tiny mistakes keep nudging it. Slowly, its "which answers are friends" map changes. Answers that sit next to each other on the clock start to feel like friends. See the stripe appear? When that stripe is strong enough, the robot can solve puzzles it never saw.

The rule of thumb

Look only at the robots that got it. The paper predicts: double the step size and double the tidy-up, and the "aha" comes 4 times sooner. The time to "aha" goes like 1 ÷ (step size × tidy-up).

Each dot is one robot. If the rule is right, the dots follow the dashed line. In the middle of the map they do: a slope of 1.0, where the rule says exactly 1. Robots near the edges of the map, where learning almost breaks, are slower than the rule says.

Chapter 6 · Being honest

Four things were only partly true

Good scientists say what didn't work too. Here's what we found.

  1. The stripe starts earlyMost of the "friends" stripe appears while the robot is still learning its homework, not only afterwards. Its strength does keep growing after, by about 28%, then stops.
  2. Step size and tidy-up aren't equalBigger steps speed up the "aha" exactly as predicted. A stronger tidy-up helps less than predicted: doubling it gives about 1.5×, not 2×. The first memorizing stage doesn't care about the tidy-up.
  3. The rule of thumb bends near the edgesIn the middle of the map, "aha" time follows 1 ÷ (step size × tidy-up) almost perfectly. Near the edges of the map, where learning nearly breaks, robots take up to 3× longer. The paper's theory actually predicts that slow-down near one edge.
  4. The robot never fully sits stillOne formula in the paper is exact when learning settles down. With a smooth robot brain it matched to 7 digits. With the jagged kind (ReLU) the paper uses, learning keeps wiggling, so it's off by about 25%.
Chapter 7 · What we actually did

From a paper to 1,890 robots

  1. Read the paperAll of it, including the appendix with the exact settings: puzzle split, robot size, step sizes, tidy-up strengths.
  2. Rebuilt the robotA small network with 17,664 knobs, written from scratch in PyTorch. 18 automatic tests check the math the theory depends on.
  3. Matched their numbersTheir example robot "gets it" at try 8,300. Ours: 8,300. Their forgetting robot peaks at 0.948 and ends at 0.204. Ours too.
  4. Trained 1,890 robotsEvery second setting from the paper's own grid, 24,000 tries each, about 4 hours on 3 computer cores.
  5. Looked insideMeasured the "friends map" over time on a 97-hour clock, and checked the leftover-mistakes formula.
  6. Wrote it all downReport, figures, code and this page, all generated from the saved results.

The same story, in research words

Pracher et al. (arXiv 2609.26679) argue that for 2-homogeneous networks trained with MSE and coupled L2 weight decay, the post-fit residual is a ridge residual that drives task-aligned growth of the empirical NTK. A one-mode reduction gives a slow clock τ = ηλs, so the grokking time scales as (ηλ)⁻¹ and the memorize/grok boundary follows η ∝ 1/λ.
