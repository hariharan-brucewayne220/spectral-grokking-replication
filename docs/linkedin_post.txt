I rebuilt a brand-new AI theory paper from its appendix, on a plain 4-core CPU, and tested whether its predictions hold.

The paper: "A Spectral Theory of Grokking" (Pracher, de Jong, Lieshaus, Jeffares, Rulands; arXiv, Sept 2026). The code isn't public yet, so I wrote everything from the paper text.

Grokking is a strange thing neural networks do. A small network learning (a + b) mod 23 gets every training example right within a few hundred steps, then sits at chance on unseen examples for thousands more. Then, with nothing changed, it suddenly gets them all right.

The paper's explanation: weight decay stops the network from fitting its training data perfectly, so a small error is left over. That leftover error slowly reshapes the network's features toward the task's hidden structure (waves over the numbers 0 to 22) until it generalizes. The headline prediction: the delay scales as 1 / (learning rate × weight decay).

What I found:
• The exact numbers printed in the paper reproduced to the digit. Their example run first hits 90% held-out accuracy at step 8,300. Mine: 8,300.
• Across {N_RUNS} networks, the boundary between "memorizes" and "groks" follows learning rate = A / weight decay, with A within {A_PCT}% of the paper's value.
• Grokking time vs 1/(learning rate × weight decay) has a log-log slope of {SLOPE} (theory: 1).
• Not everything held up cleanly. Learning rate and weight decay don't act symmetrically: raising the learning rate speeds grokking exactly as predicted, while doubling weight decay gives only ~1.5x. And a formula the paper proves for the leftover error matches to 7 digits on a smooth network, but is off by ~25% on the ReLU networks actually used, because gradient descent never fully settles there.

Three things I'd tell anyone attempting a replication:
1. Match the printed numbers before testing the big claims. If you can't hit 8,300, nothing downstream means anything.
2. Test your metrics on a case where the answer should be zero. Two of mine were quietly measuring the diagonal of a matrix instead of the structure I cared about.
3. Report what didn't reproduce. The partial results taught me more than the clean ones.
